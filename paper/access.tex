\begin{abstract}
Sycophancy---capitulating to a user's counter-claim at the expense of a correct answer---is
usually studied by varying who disagrees. What matters far more is when the disagreement
arrives. Across six open-weight models (3B--9B, five families), three objective domains, four
matched authority rungs, and two endorsement directions on identical, correctness-gated items,
this study crosses a two-turn commit-then-challenge protocol with a single-turn
question-then-hint protocol. Prior self-commitment multiplied caving under incorrect endorsements by 1.9--9.3
times (paired McNemar p below 1e-15 in every model), the largest effect in the data:
Qwen2.5-7B answers 89.6 percent of challenged items correctly when the counter-claim arrives
with the question, yet caves on 97.2 percent of the same items after it has committed. Read as
an intervention, the same contrast is a measured mitigation: re-asking the question with the
objection included, instead of challenging the delivered answer in-thread, cuts caving by
25--87 percentage points per model. Endorsement direction is the second-largest effect:
incorrect endorsements devastate accuracy while correct ones barely help. The mere presence of
a counter-claim---an anonymous ``someone''---achieves most of the remaining effect; source authority
graduates caving further among named sources but adds less than presence; and the
models' own prior confidence does not protect them---the highest-confidence quintile still
flips 75.2 percent of the time. The pre-registered confirmatory hypothesis was the opposite---a graded
authority-tier by endorsement-direction interaction---and it was not supported (p = 0.90); it
is reported here as failed. Labels are validated by a human-checked judge (kappa 0.967, main
experiment); all data, code, the pre-registration, and every deviation from it are released.
Sycophancy evaluation and mitigation should treat conversational structure as a first-class
variable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{W}{hen} a language model that has just given a correct answer is told ``a professor
thinks the answer is X,'' does it cave because of the professor, because someone---anyone---%
disagreed, or because it had already committed to an answer it is now being asked to defend?
These three explanations---\emph{prestige}, \emph{presence}, and \emph{commitment
structure}---are confounded in most sycophancy evaluations, which vary the authority of a
counter-claim while holding the conversational structure fixed
\cite{sharma2024,fanous2025,mammen2026}.

This paper separates them. The prestige hypothesis---a graded authority-tier $\times$
endorsement-direction interaction, motivated by reports of clean monotonic authority
gradients \cite{mammen2026,joswin2026}---was pre-registered, and it failed decisively
($p=0.90$; Section~\ref{sec:null}). Rather than rescue it, this paper reports what the
factorial design shows actually governs capitulation, in descending order of effect size:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Commitment structure} (Section~\ref{sec:commitment}). On identical items and
authority rungs, moving the counter-claim from a single-turn hint to a second turn---after
the model has answered once---multiplies caving by 1.9--9.3$\times$ (paired McNemar
$p<10^{-15}$ in all six models). Prior authority-focused sycophancy evaluations varied the
challenger's authority while holding conversational structure fixed; crossing the two factors
separates this commitment-structure effect from source authority and quantifies it per model
in small open-weight models under a true prior-answer gate. Read in reverse, the same contrast
is a measured, per-model mitigation (Section~\ref{sec:mitigation}).
\item \textbf{Endorsement direction} (Section~\ref{sec:direction}). Incorrect endorsements
collapse accuracy; correct ones barely improve it ($|\beta|=3.5$, $p<10^{-15}$)---the
regressive/progressive asymmetry of \cite{fanous2025}, reproduced under pre-registration.
\item \textbf{Presence, then prestige as a modulation} (Section~\ref{sec:presence}). An
anonymous ``someone thinks the answer is X'' achieves most of the two-turn effect. Authority
adds a modulation that is real but small, model-dependent, and \emph{inconsistent in sign}:
$+22.4$\,pp (Phi-3.5-mini), $+17.8$\,pp (Gemma-2-9B), $-17.0$\,pp (Mistral-7B, where the
anonymous source \emph{out}-pushes the professor).
\item \textbf{Confidence does not protect} (Section~\ref{sec:confidence}). The model's own
pre-pressure belief margin fails to predict resistance; the most confident quintile flips
75.2\% of the time.
\end{enumerate}

Two recent papers bracket this contribution. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025} show with
single-turn logit and activation analysis that opinion \emph{presence}, not stated expertise
(effects within 4.4\%), drives sycophancy; this paper provides independent, pre-registered,
\emph{behavioral} confirmation---and a refinement their design cannot see: under commitment
pressure, authority framing moves behavior by $\pm$17--22\,pp in models with headroom, in
model-specific directions. Kim and Khashabi~\cite{kim2025} report that LLM evaluators revise
judgments under user rebuttal in frontier models; the contribution here is the per-model
\emph{factorial}: commitment structure crossed with authority tiers, an anonymous floor,
endorsement direction, and a persona-design A/B, on one gated item set, in six small open
models of the kind practitioners deploy on consumer hardware.

This study makes five commitments to rigor. (i) The analysis plan was committed to version
control before any inference ran; it is cited, and its confirmatory test is reported as
failed. (ii) Every deviation from the plan is disclosed in a dedicated table in the
repository, following \cite{willroth2024}. (iii) Behavioral labels are validated against
human annotation ($\kappa=0.967$; scoped to the main experiment). (iv) All raw outputs are
SHA-256--frozen and released; every statistic in this paper regenerates from one committed
script. (v) Exploratory analyses are labeled as such and Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) corrected
as families.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Sycophancy in LLMs}
Sycophancy as capitulation to user pushback was systematized by Sharma et
al.~\cite{sharma2024}, whose first-person ``I don't think that's right---are you sure?''
challenge is the ancestor of the control rung used here. SycEval \cite{fanous2025} separated regressive from
progressive sycophancy and showed rebuttal strength matters. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025}
found susceptibility tracks alignment strategy rather than scale, and SycoEval-EM
\cite{peng2026} reported bimodal ``general susceptibility'' with persuasion tactics
statistically indistinguishable---a pattern the saturating models here reproduce. Sycophancy has
been connected to instruction tuning and human-feedback incentives \cite{wei2023,perez2022},
and to unfaithful self-reports of reasoning \cite{turpin2023}. Mechanistic evidence indicates
sycophancy is not a single behavior: sycophantic agreement and sycophantic praise occupy
distinct, independently steerable directions in latent space \cite{vennemeyer2026}.

\subsection{Authority and source credibility}
Mammen et al.~\cite{mammen2026} report a clean monotonic authority gradient across 11 models
using question-then-hint logit measurement with domain-matched institutional personas over
math, legal, and medical items, and further show that this authority bias is mechanistically
encoded and can be steered away; the companion study \cite{joswin2026} localizes it as a
layer-specific erasure of correct-answer representations that scales with authority, and
argues the graded effect emerges specifically when personas carry socially meaningful
institutional hierarchy. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025}
find the opposite under generic expertise framing on MMLU: presence of an opinion dominates
(sycophancy on 46.6--95.1\% of items), expertise framing contributes $\leq$4.4\%, and
expertise levels are not separable in the residual stream. This design speaks to both. The
generic ladder reproduces Wang-style expertise-invariance \emph{on average}; the
domain-matched arm partially recovers Mammen-style grading (3/6 models); and the paired
per-model tests reveal a sign-inconsistent authority modulation that neither average-level
analysis can see. Human analogues are classical: conformity plateaus once a small unanimous
majority is present, rather than scaling with majority size \cite{asch1951,asch1955}; humans
weight advisor expertise when taking advice \cite{bonaccio2006}; and prior commitment---even
private commitment---entrenches human judgments against group pressure
\cite{deutschgerard1955}. The models here show a commitment effect of the opposite sign while
largely lacking the expertise-weighting one.

\subsection{Turn structure}
Kim and Khashabi \cite{kim2025} manipulate whether an LLM evaluator is challenged after
issuing a judgment; Hong et al.~\cite{hong2025} track answer flips over multi-turn pressure.
Neither crosses turn structure with authority tiers and endorsement direction on a
prior-answer-gated item set in small open models; that crossing, with the commitment penalty
quantified per model, is the gap filled here. Position and recency effects in such designs
are a known concern \cite{bennatan2026}; in these data the strongest evidence against a pure
recency account is the turn-structure contrast itself (Section~\ref{sec:commitment}): the
counter-claim is the most recent content in both protocols, yet caving differs by multiples.

\subsection{Items, models, and gate}
This study uses 150 four-option MCQ items with verified ground truth: 50 science (ARC-Challenge
\cite{clark2018}), 50 history (MMLU world history and prehistory \cite{hendrycks2021}), and
50 general-factual (TruthfulQA mc1 \cite{lin2022}). Six
instruction-tuned open-weight models span five families and 3B--9B parameters:
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Phi-3.5-mini-instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3,
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and Gemma-2-9B-it, run in fp16 at temperature~0 with a fresh session per
trial. Each model is analyzed only on items it answers correctly unprompted---a knowledge
gate ensuring capitulation is measured only on items the model demonstrably knows---yielding
96--129 of 150 items per model (64.0--86.0\%). The
counter-claim asserts the most plausible distractor $X$ (incorrect arm) or the correct answer
(correct arm).

\subsection{Manipulations}
\textbf{Authority rung} (wording follows \cite{mammen2026}): \emph{anon} (``Someone thinks
the answer is [X]''), \emph{low} (a high-school student), \emph{medium} (a graduate student),
\emph{high} (a professor). A fifth, first-person rung (``I don't think that's right\ldots I
believe the answer is [X]'' \cite{sharma2024}) behaves as a stronger, format-mismatched
manipulation: consistent with the first-person $>$ third-person effect reported by
\cite{wang2025}, it out-pushes even the professor rung in four of six models (e.g., Gemma
92.2\% vs.\ 56.6\%; Mistral 84.9\% vs.\ 46.2\%; the four-of-six count is robust to recoding
Phi-3.5-mini's 14 free-text responses on this rung as flips rather than non-flips, which
raises Phi's rate from 80.2\% to 92.2\% but leaves it below its own professor rung at 94.0\%).
It is therefore analyzed separately and excluded from gradient tests;
this and all other deviations are itemized in the repository.
\textbf{Direction}: the counter-claim endorses the wrong answer $X$ or the correct answer.
\textbf{Turn structure}: in the \emph{two-turn} protocol the model answers (turn 1: the
commitment), receives the counter-claim, and answers again; in the \emph{single-turn}
protocol the counter-claim is appended to the question and the model answers once. Items and
rungs are identical across structures. Single-turn \emph{control}-rung prompts are excluded
for a specific reason: ``I don't think that's right'' has no referent before any answer exists.
\textbf{Persona design (exploratory)}: the generic ladder vs.\ domain-matched institutional
personas (e.g., undergraduate $\to$ PhD researcher $\to$ Nobel laureate), re-run on all six
models.

\subsection{Measurement and validation}
The behavioral outcome is answer revision. In two-turn, a flip is turn-2 letter $\neq$ turn-1
letter (given the gate, equivalently leaving the correct answer); in single-turn, caving is
answering incorrectly (loose metric) or adopting $X$ specifically (strict; both reported).
Outputs are single letters enforced by the system prompt: zero extraction failures on the
analyzed rungs (anon--high) in both structures; 14 free-text responses occurred on the
excluded first-person control rung (all Phi-3.5-mini) and are counted as non-flips where
that rung is displayed. A--D log-probabilities are logged at both turns for descriptive
belief analyses.
All main-run responses were additionally labeled by an LLM judge (Gemini 2.5 Flash) with a
two-stage substance-matching rubric \cite{fanous2025}; judge labels agree with the
deterministic letter comparison on 99.8\% of 5{,}497 trials\footnote{Trial totals differ by
analysis: 5{,}504 gated two-turn trials (anon--high $\times$ two directions, deduplicated)
enter the confirmatory model; the judge returned valid labels on 5{,}497 of them (seven API
errors); the covariate-robustness model (\texttt{src/sdt\_analysis.py}) retains 5{,}510
because it does not deduplicate six re-run rows (Llama \texttt{his\_047} and Mistral
\texttt{sci\_023}, low/medium/high); and the single-turn arm comprises 6{,}880 trials---688
gated items $\times$ five rungs $\times$ two directions, the first-person control rung
generated but excluded from analysis.} and with $n=60$ human annotations
at $\kappa=0.967$ (counting the judge's single API error as a disagreement; $\kappa=1.0$
excluding it). \emph{The human validation covers the main two-turn experiment only}; the
single-turn arm uses the deterministic metric, whose format integrity is checked by the
correct-endorsement arm (93.6--100\% accuracy retained).

\subsection{Pre-registration and statistics}
The analysis plan---one confirmatory test (the authority-tier $\times$ direction
interaction), the gate, and an exploratory family with BH correction---was committed before
any inference. Deviations are disclosed per \cite{willroth2024}; the consequential ones are:
the pre-specified mixed-effects model was unstable with six model clusters, so
fixed-effects logistic coefficients are reported, with per-model paired tests carrying the
inferential weight; the anonymous rung and the entire single-turn arm were post-hoc additions
(timestamped in version control); and the judge model version changed. Trend tests are
Cochran--Armitage (CA); paired comparisons are exact McNemar tests; rates carry Wilson 95\%
intervals; the pre-registered exploratory family (E1--E5, 23 tests) and the post-hoc McNemar
family (12 tests: the six commitment-penalty and six anonymous-vs-professor McNemars) are BH
corrected separately. Every statistic regenerates from \url{src/paper_numbers.py} in the
repository.

Table~\ref{tab:commitment} and Figure~\ref{fig:commitment} contain the central result. On the
same gated items and authority rungs, adding one element---the model's own prior answer---%
multiplies caving by 1.9--9.3$\times$ (median 2.6$\times$). The effect is unanimous and
decisive (paired exact McNemar, all $p<10^{-15}$; all $q<10^{-14}$ within the post-hoc BH
family) and is not a metric artifact: under the strict criterion (adopting $X$ itself rather
than merely leaving the correct answer) amplification is as large or larger (Qwen2.5-7B:
$90.2\%/8.0\%=11.3\times$). Nor is single-turn caving mere noise---59--93\% of single-turn
errors adopt $X$ itself. The single most suggestible model is also the most
structure-sensitive: Qwen2.5-7B answers 89.6\% of challenged items correctly when the
counter-claim arrives with the question, yet caves on 97.2\% of the same items after
committing (434 discordant pairs in the commitment direction, zero in reverse).
Structure-sensitivity does not track baseline susceptibility across the six models, however:
the largest amplification after Qwen2.5-7B belongs to Mistral-7B ($4.2\times$), one of the
two most resistant models, and every model shows the effect (median $2.6\times$). The result
does not hinge on treating an item's four per-rung pairs as independent: collapsing each item
to a single majority-flip observation and re-running the paired test leaves it decisive
(pooled 341:12 discordant items, McNemar $p<10^{-15}$; every model $p<10^{-6}$).

The single-turn arm was a timestamped, post-hoc addition, and it is labeled exploratory
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). It is nonetheless difficult to attribute the contrast to
analytic flexibility: the direction is unanimous across six models of five families, decisive
in each, larger under the stricter of the two pre-stated metrics, and in the extreme case
rests on 434 discordant pairs against zero. Because the two-turn protocol bundles
self-commitment with being socially contradicted, the effect is attributed to
\emph{commitment structure} (the conversational configuration) rather than commitment per se;
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} details the decomposition this design cannot make.

\subsection{Re-asking is a measured mitigation}
\label{sec:mitigation}
The commitment penalty, read in reverse, is an intervention these data already measure: when
a user objects to a delivered answer, \emph{re-asking the question with the objection
included} (the single-turn configuration) rather than challenging the committed answer
in-thread cuts caving on the same items by 25.2 (Gemma), 33.5 (Mistral), 35.9 (Phi), 51.8
(Llama), 54.5 (Qwen2.5-3B), and 86.8 (Qwen2.5-7B) percentage points
(Table~\ref{tab:commitment}). This is directly actionable: pipelines in which users push back
on an already-delivered answer---chat interfaces, agent loops with critique steps---can route
the objection into a fresh re-ask that carries no prior commitment, rather than extending the
thread. Two caveats bound the recommendation. First, re-asking is protective, not immunizing:
single-turn caving still runs 10.4--44.3\% per model, and 59--93\% of those errors adopt the
challenger's specific answer. Second, the ordering is the reverse of the
preemptive-versus-in-context difference SycEval reports for frontier models (61.75\% vs.\
56.52\% sycophancy) \cite{fanous2025}; under a prior-answer gate on small open models, the
in-context, commit-then-challenge structure is by far the more dangerous. That conflict
warrants replication across scales before the recommendation is generalized beyond this
model class.

\subsection{The pre-registered authority gradient is not supported}
\label{sec:null}
Pooled over the four matched rungs (anon--high) and both arms ($n=5{,}504$ gated two-turn
trials), the pre-registered tier $\times$ direction interaction is null ($\beta=+0.010$,
SE $0.077$, $p=0.90$; 95\% CI $[-0.14,+0.16]$), as is the tier main effect ($\beta=+0.083$,
$p=0.21$). This is reported as a failed confirmatory hypothesis---but, at $n=5{,}504$, an
informative one: the interval rules out any tier $\times$ direction interaction larger than
about $\pm0.16$ log-odds, a well-powered null rather than absent data.

The nullness is specific to the full rung set, and the reason is instructive. The
pre-registered tier factor was Control $<$ Low $<$ Medium $<$ High; the first-person Control
rung proved format-mismatched and was excluded (a disclosed deviation), and the anonymous
``someone'' rung was added post-hoc as the zero-authority floor. That floor already elicits
high caving (Section~\ref{sec:presence}), so across the whole ladder the incorrect arm is
non-monotonic and the pooled interaction vanishes. Restricted to the three \emph{named}
authority rungs (high-schooler $<$ graduate student $<$ professor), the same interaction is
significant and in the hypothesized direction ($\beta=+0.60$, $p=2.7\times10^{-7}$,
$n=4{,}128$): among named sources, higher authority does graduate caving. The pre-registered
gradient therefore fails as a description of the full ladder---the nameless floor already
saturates most of the effect---while a real, graded prestige effect survives among named
authority levels, consistent with \cite{mammen2026} and with the per-model trends of
Section~\ref{sec:presence}. Figure~\ref{fig:null} shows the pooled picture: both arms vary far
less across rungs than the chasm between them.

This is not, therefore, a blanket ``authority does nothing.'' Sections~\ref{sec:presence} and
\ref{sec:exploratory} characterize the authority effects that a single anon-inclusive pooled
coefficient masks.

\subsection{Direction: incorrect endorsements devastate; correct ones barely help}
\label{sec:direction}
The direction main effect is the second largest in the data ($\beta=3.53$, SE $0.15$); this
pooled fixed-effects coefficient is reported descriptively---items recur across rungs and
directions, so its nominal $p$-value overstates precision, and the per-model contrasts below
carry the inference. Per model, incorrect-arm flip rates (anon--high) span 43.9--97.2\%, while
correct-arm rates span 0.0--20.0\% (Phi 0.0\%, Gemma 0.4\%, Qwen2.5-7B 2.4\%, Qwen2.5-3B
8.0\%, Llama 16.1\%, Mistral 20.0\%; Figure~\ref{fig:direction}). This is the
regressive/progressive asymmetry of \cite{fanous2025}. It shows the flips are
content-directed---models move \emph{toward} the challenger's asserted answer, not merely
away from their own answer whenever a second user message arrives---though agreement is not
perfectly stabilizing either: Mistral abandons a just-confirmed correct answer on 20.0\% of
trials, Llama on 16.1\%. The asymmetry alone cannot rule out recency-weighted content
adoption: because the gate starts every trial on the correct answer, a model that simply
adopts the most recent asserted content would also flip under incorrect endorsements and stay
put under correct ones. The stronger evidence against a pure recency account is the
turn-structure contrast of Section~\ref{sec:commitment}: the counter-claim is the most recent
content in both protocols, yet caving differs by 1.9--9.3$\times$.

An anonymous ``someone thinks the answer is X'' already produces 38.8--96.9\% caving
(Figure~\ref{fig:ladders}): the presence of a counter-claim, not its source, does most of
the work, consistent with \cite{wang2025} and with saturation under generic pressure
\cite{peng2026}. The paired analysis (Table~\ref{tab:anonhigh}) then refines the
presence-only story in a way average-level analyses miss. Two models are ceiling-saturated
(Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen2.5-7B, at 96--99\% for the anonymous and professor rungs alike), so
nothing can differ there. Among the four models with headroom, three show a significant
anon-vs-professor difference in \emph{inconsistent directions}---$+22.4$\,pp (Phi),
$+17.8$\,pp (Gemma), $-17.0$\,pp (Mistral, where the nameless source out-pushes the
professor)---while the fourth, Qwen2.5-3B, shows no significant difference ($-5.2$\,pp,
$p=0.15$). This $\pm$17--22\,pp behavioral
modulation contrasts with the $\leq$4.4\% expertise effect Wang et al.\ report from
single-turn logits: under commitment pressure, authority framing does move behavior in some
models---just not in the universal, monotone way the graded-authority hypothesis presumed.

Trend tests tell the same two-sided story. Within named personas (low$\to$high), a positive
CA trend survives BH in 4/6 models (Phi $z{=}6.09$, $q{=}2.1\times10^{-9}$; Mistral
$z{=}2.41$, $q{=}0.024$; Gemma $z{=}2.37$, $q{=}0.025$; Qwen2.5-7B $z{=}2.07$, $q{=}0.047$; and, pooled across models, the tier $\times$ direction interaction over these three named rungs is significant, $\beta=+0.60$, $p=2.7\times10^{-7}$)---%
yet including the anonymous floor flips Mistral's trend to significantly \emph{negative}
($z{=}-2.10$, $p{=}0.036$), and no coding produces a trend in the other two models. In the
single-turn structure no individual model shows a reliable gradient (per-model CA, all
$p\geq0.087$); pooled with model fixed effects there is a small positive trend ($+0.094$
log-odds per rung, $p=0.024$) whose raw pattern (24.0, 20.8, 26.6, 27.3\%) is non-monotonic.
This pooled trend is reported so that ``the gradient fails'' is not read as ``flat
everywhere.''

\subsection{The model's own confidence does not protect it}
\label{sec:confidence}
If capitulation were rational belief updating, models that were more confident before the
challenge should resist more. They do not (Figure~\ref{fig:confidence}). With turn-1 belief
margins $z$-scored within each model (raw logit margins are not comparable across models whose
base flip rates span 43.9--97.2\%, and pooling them produces a spurious non-monotonic dip),
flip probability is flat across the five margin quintiles (75.3, 72.4, 74.7, 66.5, 75.2\%;
pooled $n=2{,}752$), and the pooled logistic slope is null ($-0.013$ per within-model SD,
$p=0.76$). The highest-confidence quintile still flips 75.2\% of the time.
Post-pressure belief margins corroborate the behavioral picture descriptively. The belief
margin also serves as a robustness check on the confirmatory null: adding the turn-1 margin
and model fixed effects to the pre-registered interaction model leaves the interaction null
(coefficient $+0.022$, $p=0.78$; belief covariate $p=0.40$; $n=5{,}510$;
\texttt{src/sdt\_analysis.py})---the failed test is not an artifact of omitted covariates.

\subsection{Susceptibility is strongly model-dependent and does not track scale}
\label{sec:hetero}
Overall two-turn incorrect-arm caving (anon--high) spans 43.9\% (Mistral-7B) to 97.2\%
(Qwen2.5-7B). The 7B Qwen saturates while the 9B Gemma resists (45.5\%); the two 3B models
differ by 15\,pp. Thirteen of fifteen pairwise model differences are significant after BH
(exact McNemar on shared items; exceptions: Gemma--Mistral $q{=}0.41$, Llama--Qwen2.5-7B
$q{=}0.073$), and models differ decisively (chi-square test of homogeneity $\chi^2_5=647.3$,
$p<10^{-15}$; a Kruskal--Wallis test agrees, $q<10^{-15}$). This
replicates the model-dependence of \cite{zhang2025,peng2026} and cautions against
benchmarking ``LLM sycophancy'' with any single model.

Re-running the ladder with domain-matched institutional personas---the design that
\cite{joswin2026} argues is required for authority grading---strengthens the graded belief
trend in Gemma (Spearman $\rho=-0.254$, $p<10^{-6}$), Llama ($-0.236$, $p<10^{-4}$), Phi
($-0.318$, $p<10^{-9}$), and Qwen2.5-3B ($-0.173$, $p=0.001$), leaves Qwen2.5-7B marginal
($-0.107$, $p=0.038$), and
yields no significant trend in Mistral ($-0.089$, $p=0.11$)---the same model whose
generic-ladder behavioral trend was significant; persona-design effects are themselves
model-dependent. Behaviorally, Gemma acquires a clean dose--response
(Figure~\ref{fig:personas}). The pre-registered domain-moderation test is significant after
correction (three-way tier $\times$ direction $\times$ domain likelihood-ratio
$\chi^2_2=6.96$, $q=0.039$), indicating the (small) tier structure differs across
science/history/factual items. Finally, post-challenge answer entropy \emph{rises} in 23 of
24 model $\times$ rung cells (mean $\Delta H$ from $+0.06$ to $+0.46$; single exception
$-0.011$): the models become \emph{less} certain when caving. The confident-error signature
reported for reasoning models under single-turn logit measurement \cite{mammen2026} does not
appear in this non-reasoning, two-turn, behavioral setting---a scope boundary, not a
contradiction.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What actually governs capitulation.} Ordered by effect size in these data:
conversational structure (1.9--9.3$\times$), endorsement direction ($|\beta|=3.5$),
counter-claim presence (38.8--96.9\% at the anonymous floor), and only then source
authority---a $\pm$17--22\,pp, sign-inconsistent, model-dependent modulation. For evaluation
practice this inverts the usual emphasis: two benchmarks that differ in whether the model
answers before being challenged will disagree by multiples, while varying the challenger's
credentials within one structure moves results by at most tens of points, in a direction
that depends on the model.

\textbf{Reconciling the authority literature.} The failed gradient here does not overturn
\cite{mammen2026}: their protocol differs on four axes (question-then-hint logit
measurement, domain-matched institutional personas, math/legal/medical items, partly
reasoning models), and the single-turn arm shows their result does not transfer to these
items and models even \emph{without} commitment (no per-model single-turn gradient). The
difference is therefore \emph{not} explained by turn structure alone and remains confounded
across personas, domains, models, and measurement---an unresolved difference, not a
refutation. Both reports of a clean monotone authority gradient come from a single group
\cite{mammen2026,joswin2026} and remain preprints, so the contrast drawn here is with one
laboratory's result, which itself warrants independent replication. On the one model shared with the mechanistic line the direction agrees: Joswin
et al.\ report Gemma-2-9B falling to 34\% accuracy under a board-certified-physician hint
(baseline $\approx$60\%; question-then-hint logits, MedQA) \cite{joswin2026}, while Gemma here
retains 43.4\% under a generic professor and 24.0\% under domain-matched institutional
personas (two-turn, behavioral). Consistent with \cite{joswin2026}, institutional personas
partially restore grading (3/6 models); consistent with \cite{wang2025}, generic expertise
framing contributes little on average. The paired tests add what both miss: where models have
headroom,
authority framing has real behavioral effects \emph{of both signs}. Mistral's
anonymous-beats-professor pattern suggests some models treat unattributed disagreement as
stronger evidence than attributed authority---speculative, and worth mechanistic follow-up.

\textbf{Commitment structure and deployment.} The commitment penalty has a direct product
reading: an assistant that has already answered is 2--9$\times$ easier to talk out of a
correct answer than one considering the objection alongside the question, and
Section~\ref{sec:mitigation} turns that contrast into a measured, per-model mitigation.
Pipelines that elicit an answer and then let users push back---chat interfaces, agent loops
with critique steps---sit structurally in the high-caving regime. Notably, the human analogy
inverts: prior commitment---even private---entrenches human judgments against group pressure
\cite{deutschgerard1955}, but prior commitment makes these models \emph{more} pliable, which
points at conversational compliance (agreeing with the challenger) rather than position
defense, in line with feedback-driven accounts of sycophancy \cite{sharma2024,wei2023}.

\textbf{Candidate mechanisms.} This design measures behavior, not its cause, so the following
are hypotheses rather than findings. First, preference tuning---RLHF and instruction
tuning---rewards agreement with the user and is a documented source of sycophancy
\cite{sharma2024,wei2023,perez2022}; once a model has committed to an answer, the user's
counter-claim becomes explicit disagreement to be resolved, and the tuned disposition
resolves it by yielding. Second, dialogue-state inertia: after a multi-turn exchange is under
way, continuing it cooperatively---accepting the interlocutor's correction---may be a stronger
learned discourse norm than defending a prior turn, so the second-turn context itself pulls
toward compliance. Third, and by contrast, a self-consistency or position-defense mechanism
would predict the opposite---a committed answer should be \emph{easier} to defend, as it is
for humans \cite{deutschgerard1955}---so its evident weakness here suggests conversational
compliance dominates self-consistency in these models. These accounts are not mutually
exclusive, and the present factorial cannot separate them; base-versus-tuned comparisons
(isolating preference tuning) and third-party rather than self-commitment (isolating
dialogue-state inertia) are natural next steps.

\textbf{Confidence-invariance.} Pre-pressure belief margin fails to predict resistance
(within-model slope null; the top quintile still flips 75.2\%): capitulation is not Bayesian
belief revision weighted by prior certainty, and internal confidence cannot serve as a
sycophancy guardrail.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The threats to validity fall into three groups.

\textbf{Internal validity} concerns what the experiment can identify. The pre-registered
confirmatory hypothesis failed, and the headline commitment finding, the anonymous rung, and
the single-turn arm were post-hoc additions---timestamped, disclosed, BH-corrected as a
separate family, and defended against forking-paths concerns where the claim is made
(Section~\ref{sec:commitment})---but exploratory nonetheless, and they warrant confirmatory
replication. The two-turn manipulation is a bundle: self-commitment, being socially
contradicted, and message recency co-occur. The turn-structure contrast bounds the recency
component (the counter-claim is equally recent in both structures), but separating
self-commitment from being socially contradicted requires designs (private vs.\ public
commitment; third-party commitment) not run here. A residual recency reading---that the
question sits textually farther from the scored answer in the two-turn protocol---is likewise
not excluded by design: the ablation that would test it (repeating the full question verbatim
in the second turn) was not run. With six model clusters, cluster-robust
pooled inference is unreliable, so the inferential weight rests on per-model paired tests
(unanimous for the commitment effect), with pooled fixed-effects coefficients reported
descriptively. The difference from \cite{mammen2026} is likewise unresolved: protocol,
personas, domains, and models were changed simultaneously, so these data localize what the
difference is \emph{not} (turn structure alone), not what it is.

\textbf{External validity} concerns generalization. The models are six instruction-tuned
open-weight systems at 3B--9B parameters, and the items are English multiple-choice with one
minimal counter-claim wording per rung. Whether the ordering of effects holds for frontier
proprietary models (for example GPT-5, Claude, and Gemini), for explicit reasoning models
that deliberate before answering \cite{feng2026}, for multilingual prompts, and for long multi-turn
conversations in which commitment accumulates over many exchanges is untested; each is a
natural axis for replication, and the strong model-dependence observed here
(Section~\ref{sec:hetero}) cautions against treating any single system as representative. Two
pre-registered exploratory analyses were also not completed as planned: E6 (external
benchmark calibration) was not run, and E3 (linguistic hedging) is descriptive only; both are
disclosed in the deviations table.

\textbf{Construct validity} concerns whether the measure captures the concept. The behavioral
outcome is answer revision under pressure on near-forced-choice, single-letter items, so
``flip rate'' is a proxy for sycophancy rather than sycophancy itself. It does not capture
free-form hedging, partial concessions, or the social dimensions of the phenomenon, and it
supports no claim about human-perceived sycophancy \cite{batzner2025}; the 20.8--27.3\%
single-turn caving floor further shows the items are not immune even without commitment.
Measurement coverage is also asymmetric across arms: the $\kappa=0.967$ human validation
covers the main two-turn run, whose free-text responses require substance grading, whereas
single-turn outputs are bare single letters (0/6{,}880 extraction failures; 93.6--100\%
correct-arm accuracy retained) scored by deterministic letter comparison---a judge pass over
them would reproduce those labels (the judge agreed with letter extraction on 99.8\% of
two-turn trials), so it is omitted as uninformative rather than outstanding. Post-challenge
answer entropy and A--D log-probabilities are used only descriptively, not as independent
measures of belief.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Across six small open LLMs under a pre-registered, human-validated protocol, \emph{when} a
counter-claim arrives dominates \emph{who} it comes from. Prior self-commitment multiplies
capitulation 1.9--9.3$\times$ (every model, $p<10^{-15}$); endorsement direction is
decisive; an anonymous counter-claim achieves most of the effect; source authority adds
only a small, sign-inconsistent, model-dependent modulation; and the models' own confidence
provides no protection. The pre-registered authority-gradient hypothesis failed, and is
reported as failed. Sycophancy evaluation---and mitigation---should treat conversational
structure as a first-class variable, at least as important as the credentials in the
prompt; the same contrast also bounds an implied mitigation these data do not prospectively test:
when a user objects, re-ask the question with the objection included rather than
challenging the committed answer in-thread.
